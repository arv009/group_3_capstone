SecondLook

An AI-assisted second review for borderline loan declines

Group 3 · Uplift Code Camp, Python for Data and AI (Batch 3) · Final presentation, 2 December 2026 · Live app: secondlook.streamlit.app · Code: github.com/Funkchini/secondlook

1. Problem

About half of Filipino adults have no formal financial account. Lenders often decline applicants with thin credit files, and usually give no reason. Many of those applicants would have repaid.

Business question: among applicants a lender would decline, which ones are actually safe to approve, and what realistic changes would get the rest approved?

2. Data

We used the Home Credit Default Risk data from Kaggle: 307,511 past loan applicants, of whom 8.07% had payment difficulties ("default"), plus six credit-history tables (credit bureau records, previous applications, and past instalment, card and cash-loan payments). The data is anonymised and its country is not disclosed.

The data needed cleaning: 18% of employment values were a placeholder, 41 columns were over half missing, and one income was a 117-million error. We split it 80/20, stratified by outcome: 246,008 applicants to train and 61,503 to test. Every result below is on the test set, which the model never saw.

3. Approach

  1. Features. We loaded the history tables into SQLite and wrote SQL to summarise each applicant's past (loan counts, refusals, late-payment rates), then added ratios such as loan-to-income. The model uses 158 features. Gender is left out on purpose.
  2. Model. LightGBM gradient-boosted trees, compared with a logistic regression baseline and checked with 5-fold cross-validation. The cutoff (approve if the predicted default risk, PD, is below 0.17) maximises profit under stated assumptions: +15% of the loan on repayment, −60% on default. Applicants with PD 0.17–0.22 are flagged under review instead of declined.
  3. Explain. SHAP shows which factors raised or lowered each applicant's risk, in plain English.
  4. Path to approval. For applicants not approved, we re-score a grid of smaller loans and longer terms with the real model and suggest the smallest change that would be approved. Age, gender and past defaults are never suggested.
  5. Letters. Claude Haiku 5.5 drafts a decision letter from the SHAP reasons and the path. Rule checks block invented factors and personal traits. An independent audit (a second Claude lists the letter's claims; Python checks them against the model) flags problems for the loan officer.
  6. Fairness audit. Approval and error rates by gender, age band and credit file, with 1,000-resample bootstrap intervals, plus an experiment that retrains the model without the sensitive features.
  7. Personas. K-Means clustering groups the 7,263 applicants we don't approve into five borrower personas.
  8. AI Analyst. A hand-written agent loop: Claude answers a manager's question by writing read-only SQL and calling SecondLook's own tools, and shows its work.

Everything is in a public Streamlit app with five pages: Home, Portfolio Dashboard (with Personas), Applicant Review, Model Card and AI Analyst.

5. Fairness findings

  • The model is calibrated in every group (predicted risk within 1 point of the real default rate), so one cutoff means the same risk for everyone.
  • It passes the four-fifths check: no group's approval rate is below 0.8 of the best group's. Ages 20–29 come closest, at 0.82.
  • But good borrowers are not approved equally. Repayers aged 20–29 are approved 83.1% of the time, against 97.2% at ages 60–69 (a gap of 14.1 points, 95% interval 13.2–15.0). Men and thin-file repayers are also approved less often (gaps of 4.2 and 5.3 points). Every interval excludes zero.
  • Removing the sensitive features made it worse. Without age, the model overstates young applicants' risk (11.9% predicted vs 11.3% actual), the age gap widens to 16.3 points, and ages 20–29 fall below the four-fifths line (0.80).
  • Our decision: keep the full model. Sensitive traits are never given as reasons, borderline applicants, where young, thin-file and male applicants are over-represented, get a human second look, and declined applicants get a path to approval.

6. Limitations

  • Decision support only. A loan officer makes every decision and reviews every letter.
  • Unknown country. The model must be validated on Philippine applicants before any real use.
  • Outcomes exist only for past approved loans, so we cannot see how applicants the lender declined would have behaved.
  • SHAP explains the model, not the world. A reason is what drove the score, not a proven cause.
  • The letter audit is noisy: about half its flags are false alarms, and verdicts agree only 73% of the time between runs. Letters are respectful but low on empathy.
  • The AI Analyst was tested on ten questions we wrote. Vaguer questions will be less reliable, so every answer shows its SQL and tool calls.
  • Profit uses illustrative assumptions. Intersections of groups (for example, young men with thin files) were not audited.

Next steps, if continued: validate on local data, audit intersections of groups, improve letter empathy, and monitor calibration as the applicant mix changes.
